# 01. Introduction to AI Fluency

Video: ~4 min · Module time: 10 to 15 min
Presenters: Joseph Feller (Cork University Business School), Drew Bent (teacher, programmer, member of technical staff at Anthropic), Rick Dakan (Ringling College of Art and Design), Maggie Vo (education research team, Anthropic)

## Learning objectives
- Understand the purpose and structure of the course
- Recognise why AI Fluency matters today
- Identify the key components of the learning journey ahead
- Set clear expectations for what the course will give you

## What the course is (and is not) about
- The course is **not really about AI as a technology**. It is about **us humans** and how we interact and collaborate with AI systems.
- The aim is to imagine AI as more than a "next-level spell checker": as a **trusted partner for creative and innovative problem solving**, and to approach that confidently and capably.
- The AI world moves very fast. Without a **grounding framework** it is hard to know where to start or keep up, or to get past "the next prompt engineering fad". That is the reason the course exists.
- It is explicitly **not** a collection of technical definitions or prompting tips "that may be outdated next month".
- Core goal: **change how we think *about* AI so that we can learn how to think *with* AI.**

## Definition: AI Fluency
> The ability to engage with AI systems in ways that are **effective, efficient, ethical, and safe**.

## Why now
- Over the last few years AI went from specialised technology to interactive systems that millions use daily at school, work and home.
- This creates both **opportunity and uncertainty**. The presenters observe:
  - organisations adopting AI **without clear strategies**
  - people **growing frustrated** with systems they do not fully understand
  - a **widening gap between what is possible and what feels comfortable and intuitive**
- The course is meant to help bridge that gap.

## Course structure (the journey)
1. Overview of the **AI Fluency Framework**: a model of human-AI interaction the authors found useful and powerful. It covers the *ways* we interact with AI and the **four core competencies (the 4Ds)** that make those interactions richer.
2. A closer look at each competency, each framed as a question:
   - **Delegation**: when should humans do the work, and when should AI?
   - **Description**: how do we communicate clearly with AI systems?
   - **Discernment**: how do we evaluate what AI gives us?
   - **Diligence**: how do we make sure our interaction with AI is responsible, transparent and accountable?
3. Each section has focused videos plus exercises. You are encouraged to spend much of the course **actually trying things**.
4. Anthropic staff also cover: what makes today's AI different from earlier technologies, real capabilities and limitations, and practical techniques that apply the 4Ds.

## The teaching philosophy
- Most AI training focuses only on **tactical skills** (specific prompts, which settings to adjust, which system to use). Useful, but these **go out of date quickly**.
- This course focuses on **core competencies and deep understanding**, so you are prepared for today's AI *and* the changes to come.

## What you will have by the end
1. A **framework** to guide your own interactions with AI
2. **Confidence** in deciding when and how to engage AI effectively
3. **Practical skills** for more thoughtful, fluid human-AI collaboration
4. The ability to **evaluate and take responsibility** for the outcomes of those collaborations (the page phrases it as evaluating and disseminating AI-assisted work responsibly)

## Key takeaways (from the lesson page)
- The focus is human-AI **collaboration**, not just understanding AI as a technology
- AI Fluency = effective, efficient, ethical, safe
- The framework centres on the 4Ds: Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence
- The goal is **lasting** skills that stay relevant as AI evolves
- Effective collaboration needs practical skills **and** a fundamental shift in how you think about working with AI

# 02. Why do we need AI Fluency?

Video: ~6 min

## Learning objectives
- Understand what AI Fluency means and why it matters in a fast-changing landscape
- Recognise the three ways we collaborate with AI: **Automation, Augmentation, Agency**

## The problem: access is not fluency
- AI is reshaping how we communicate, create, learn and solve problems, at work and in personal life. Many people now have versatile assistants for writing, brainstorming, research, decision making and more.
- But **having powerful systems does not mean we know how to make the most of them, or how to use them responsibly.**
- Typical moments that reveal the gap between *access* and *fluency*:
  - getting an **unexpected response** and not knowing how to proceed
  - **struggling to explain** exactly what you need and leaving frustrated
  - wondering whether the **information you share is properly protected**
- The question the course asks: how do we move beyond "a few prompt tricks" to a thoughtful, responsible approach that keeps working as AI evolves?

## What AI Fluency is
- **Not** being a technical expert, and **not** memorising "the 10 best prompts" for this month's trending task.
- **Is** a collection of **practical skills, knowledge, insights and values** that reinforce each other and **adapt as technology changes**.
- At its heart: interacting with AI in ways that are **effective, efficient, ethical and safe**. Unpacked:
  - **maximise** what you get from AI interactions
  - **without wasting** time and energy
  - in an **honest and responsible** way
  - that **protects the privacy and security** of yourself and others

## The three modes of engaging with AI
From the presenters' research and personal experience. Understanding them shows why fluency must go beyond prompt engineering.

### 1. Automation
- AI **completes a specific task based on your instructions**. You define what is needed, AI executes.
- Examples: summarise a document, draft an email, create an image, plan a trip itinerary.
- **Works well** when you have a **clear outcome in mind**.
- **Harder** when you are not yet sure what you are looking for.

### 2. Augmentation
- You and AI **collaborate and complete the task together**. AI is not a machine automating a task but a **creative thinking and problem-solving partner**.
- Examples:
  - stuck developing a story character: bounce ideas back and forth, elaborate backstories, experiment with dialogue, refine the character
  - working through a hard **architectural problem** in an app you are building
  - formulating your thoughts on a complex research topic
- AI **does not do the work for you; it helps you do your work better**.
- **Works best** when solutions are not straightforward and you need **space to explore and experiment**.

### 3. Agency
- AI **works independently on your behalf**.
- Examples: an assistant that categorises incoming email by topic or urgency and starts drafting replies to the urgent ones; an ever-changing interactive experience for website visitors; a dynamically interactive character in a game.
- Key idea: rather than defining specific actions, **you establish the AI's knowledge and behaviour patterns**.
- Metaphor: you become **less like a scriptwriter giving exact directions and more like a director setting a vision**.

### Diagram: the three modes
```
 YOU DIRECT EACH STEP ◄──────────────────────────────────► AI ACTS ON ITS OWN

   AUTOMATION              AUGMENTATION               AGENCY
   "do this task"          "let's work this           "here is what you know and
                            out together"              how to behave, now go"

   You ──task──► AI        You ◄──ideas──► AI         You ──vision──► AI ──► acts
          ▼                    (back and forth)                         repeatedly
       output

   best: clear outcome     best: open-ended,          best: recurring work,
         in mind                 exploratory work           interactive experiences
                                                      you = director, not scriptwriter
```

### How the modes relate
- **None is inherently better**; they serve different purposes and excel in different situations.
- You might use **all three in one project**.
- Many people **start with automation**, but the presenters found that **augmentation and agency** take most advantage of AI's unique capabilities and **often lead to the most creative and effective solutions**.

## Bigger idea: AI is not "just a tool"
- AI can act as a **tool**, a **medium**, a **partner or co-creator**, and sometimes all at once.
- That shift from tool to collaborator gives technology a **new role** in creative and problem-solving work, which means **we have new roles too** and need new skills.
- Those skills are what the AI Fluency Framework describes: **four areas of competence** (the 4Ds) apply whichever mode you use.

## Quick self-test
- Name the three modes and give one example each.
- Which mode suits a task where you do not yet know what the outcome should look like? (Augmentation)
- In agency, what do you define instead of specific actions? (The AI's knowledge and behaviour patterns)

# 03. The 4D Framework

Video: ~5 min · Presenter: Rick Dakan (Ringling College of Art and Design)

## Learning objective
- Explain the AI Fluency Framework and its four core competencies, the **4Ds**: Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence

## Framing
- Whatever mode you are in (automation, augmentation or agency), **four competencies make all the difference** to collaborating effectively, efficiently, ethically and safely.

| D | One-line definition | Main purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Delegation** | Thoughtfully deciding what work to do with AI vs. yourself | effective + efficient |
| **Description** | Communicating clearly with AI systems | collaborative success |
| **Discernment** | Evaluating AI outputs and behaviour with a critical eye | quality, usefulness |
| **Diligence** | Interacting with AI responsibly | safe + ethical |

### Diagram: how the 4Ds fit together
```
 ┌─────────────── DILIGENCE: around everything (ethical + safe) ───────────────┐
 │                                                                             │
 │  DELEGATION ──► DESCRIPTION ──► AI output ──► DISCERNMENT                   │
 │  what goes      tell it                       is it good?                   │
 │  to AI?         clearly                            │                        │
 │      ▲              ▲                              │                        │
 │      │              └───────── refine ◄────────────┤  (the D-D loop)        │
 │      └──────────── wrong tool / approach ◄─────────┘                        │
 │                                                                             │
 └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   Delegation, Description, Discernment: mainly effective + efficient
```

## 1. Delegation: the big picture
Questions to ask:
- What are you trying to accomplish?
- What kinds of work are involved?
- What should you handle yourself, and where might AI help?

Example (research project): have the AI review lengthy documents and data, then have a thoughtful discussion with it about implications and findings, but **reserve the critical analysis and final conclusions for yourself**.

To delegate well you need to:
1. **Understand your goal** and the problem you are solving
2. **Recognise what AI can and cannot do well**
3. **Thoughtfully divide the work** between you and the AI

Delegation is **not just offloading tasks**. It is having a clear vision and **strategically choosing how AI fits into your process**. Essential for effective *and* efficient collaboration.

## 2. Description: clear communication
- Contrast: vaguely saying *"Make me a logo"* vs. describing your company's **values, target audience, preferred colours, style references**, etc.
- Tutor example: *"Don't tell me the answer, just help me work through this problem step by step so I can better understand the concept."* This describes **how you want the AI to behave**, not just what you want.
- Description goes beyond writing prompts. It means **detailed, context-rich conversations** that establish:
  - what you hope to achieve and the **format of the output**
  - **how the AI should approach** the task
  - the **context and information** it needs to work with you well
  - the **tone and style** of the interaction
- Goal: articulate your needs and vision so that **both you and the AI** are set up for collaborative success.

## 3. Discernment: evaluating what AI gives you
Example: you asked for a marketing strategy. Assess:
- Are the **facts accurate**?
- Does the **reasoning make sense**?
- Do the recommendations **align with your brand values and audience**?
- Most importantly: **does the output actually help you move forward?**

Discernment:
- draws on **your own domain expertise**
- needs judgment and critical insight to **separate the useful from the not useful**
- includes recognising when output **needs refinement** or should be **set aside entirely**

**Description-Discernment loop:** most AI interactions are small loops of *describe what you need → evaluate what you get → refine the request → repeat*. (Covered in depth in lesson 11.)

## 4. Diligence: responsible interaction
Example questions:
- Using AI to write job descriptions or review applications: how do you **ensure fairness and control for bias**?
- Making important decisions with AI help: how do you **verify the accuracy** of what it presents?
- Are you **protecting sensitive data**?
- Have you considered how to be **transparent about AI's involvement**?
- Are you **willing to be accountable** for the AI-assisted work?

Diligence = **taking ownership** of AI-assisted work and being willing to **stand behind the final product**. Critical for safe and ethical collaboration.

## Recap
- AI Fluency = practical skills, knowledge, insights and values for using AI effectively, efficiently, ethically and safely.
- Delegation decides **when and how** to use AI · Description **communicates** clearly · Discernment **evaluates** outputs · Diligence uses AI **responsibly**.
- Why they matter: they are **not tied to specific tools or techniques** that go out of date. They are fundamental skills that let you adapt as the technology evolves.
- The 4Ds apply across **all three** modes (automation, augmentation, agency).

## Exercises
**Exercise 1: Apply the 4Ds (5 min).** Pick a scenario and answer the four questions:

| Scenario | Delegation | Description | Discernment | Diligence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Communication:** AI drafts a series of marketing campaign emails | What do you handle vs. collaborate on? | How do you convey tone, purpose, success criteria? | What criteria tell you the drafts meet your needs? | What transparency/responsibility issues matter? |
| **Research:** AI helps analyse a large dataset for a paper | How do you split the analytical work? | What context about your research question does the AI need? | How do you verify its analysis? | What ethical issues arise when publishing AI-assisted research? |
| **Creative:** AI helps develop story character concepts | What do you explore with AI vs. develop alone? | How do you guide it to fit your story's world? | How do you decide what to keep, modify, discard? | How do you acknowledge AI's contribution? |

**Exercise 2: Explore something you love (5 to 10 min).** Chat naturally with Claude about a topic you know well. Notice when Claude **enhances your thinking**, when you need to **clarify or correct** it, and when **your expertise** leads you to evaluate its responses. (Tip from the page: try the course exercises with several assistants to feel how they differ.)

**Exercise 3: Learn something new (5 to 10 min).** Ask Claude to teach you an unfamiliar topic; do not worry about prompting "right". Notice helpful explanations, concrete examples, natural responses to follow-ups, and **places where you would want to double-check**. Compare with Exercise 2: discernment is much harder when you lack expertise.

# 04. Generative AI fundamentals (Deep Dive 1, part 1)

Video: ~6 min · Presenter: Drew Bent (Anthropic)

## Learning objectives
- Define generative AI and how it differs from other types of AI
- Recognise its key characteristics and technological foundations

## What generative AI is
- AI systems that **create new content** rather than just analysing existing data.
- Contrast:
  - **Traditional AI**: classifies an email as spam / not spam based on patterns. It **analyses and categorises**.
  - **Generative AI**: writes a completely new email for you. It **creates something that did not exist before**.
- This is described as a **fundamental shift in AI capabilities**.

## Large language models (LLMs)
- A prominent type of generative AI (e.g. Anthropic's Claude models).
- **"Language model"**: trained to predict and generate human language.
- **"Large"**: contains **billions of parameters**, the mathematical values that determine how the model processes information (loosely like synaptic connections in a brain).

## Three developments that made it possible
The path was not sudden; three things came together:
1. **Algorithmic and architectural breakthroughs.** Neural networks existed conceptually for decades, but the **transformer architecture (2017)** was a game changer. It excels at processing **sequences of text** while maintaining **relationships between words across long passages**, which is critical for understanding language in context.
2. **Explosion of digital data.** The raw material for training. Models like Claude learn from diverse sources such as **websites, code repositories and other text** representing human knowledge and communication, giving a broad and nuanced understanding of language and concepts.
3. **Massive increases in computing power.** Specialised hardware (**GPUs**, graphics processing units, and **TPUs**, tensor processing units) plus **distributed computing networks ("clusters")** enabled processing impossible a few years earlier.

## Scaling laws and emergent capabilities
- **Scaling laws**: empirical finding that as models get **larger**, train on **more data**, with **more compute**, performance **improves in predictable ways**.
- More surprising: **entirely new capabilities emerge** at larger scale that nobody explicitly programmed, e.g. reasoning through problems step by step, adapting to new tasks with minimal instruction.

### Diagram: from ingredients to answers
```
 INGREDIENTS   transformer (2017)  +  huge text data  +  GPU/TPU compute
                                   │
                                   ▼   bigger = predictably better (scaling laws)
 PRE-TRAINING  read billions of texts, keep predicting the next word
               result: a "map" of language and knowledge
                                   │
                                   ▼
 FINE-TUNING   learn to follow instructions; helpful, honest, harmless
               (human feedback + reinforcement learning)
                                   │
                                   ▼
 IN USE        your prompt ──► model continues the text ──► response
               (generates new text, does NOT look answers up in a database)
```

## How LLMs are trained
### Pre-training
- The model analyses patterns across **billions of text examples**. Analogy: reading every website and text you can find, not just to absorb information but to learn the **statistical relationships between words, phrases and concepts**.
- Result: something like a **complex map of language and knowledge**.
- Mechanism: show the model text and ask it to **predict what comes next**; over many iterations it refines its predictions and learns what makes language coherent and meaningful.

### Fine-tuning
- After pre-training, the model learns to **follow instructions**, give **helpful responses**, and **avoid harmful content**.
- Often involves **human feedback** plus **reinforcement learning** (rewards and penalties) to shape behaviour toward being **helpful, honest and harmless**.
- Then the model is **deployed** for people to use.

## What happens when you use it
- You provide a **prompt**: text the model reads and then **continues from**, based on patterns learned in training.
- The model is **not retrieving pre-written answers from a database**. It **generates new text** that statistically follows from what you wrote.

## The context window
- A practical limit on how much information the model can consider at once. Think of it as the AI's **working memory**.
- It includes **your prompts, the AI's responses, and anything else you have shared** in the conversation.
- Context windows keep growing (longer documents and conversations), but the limit means the model **does not have unlimited access to information** and **cannot use content outside its current context window** without specialised tools such as web search.

### Diagram: the context window
```
 CONTEXT WINDOW = the model's working memory for this conversation
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ prompt │ reply │ your file │ prompt │ reply │ prompt │ ...   │
 └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
 outside the box = invisible to the model, unless a tool (e.g. web search) brings it in
```

## The three characteristics that make modern generative AI powerful
1. **Processing vast amounts of information during training**, so it learns complex, nuanced patterns in language and knowledge
2. **In-context learning**: adapts to new tasks from instructions or examples **in your prompt**, with no extra training
3. **Emergent capabilities from scale**: abilities not explicitly designed in, sometimes surprising even their creators

- Name the three enabling developments. What year was the transformer introduced? (2017)
- Pre-training objective? (Predict the next text.) What does fine-tuning add?

# 05. Capabilities & limitations (Deep Dive 1, part 2)

Video: ~7 min · Presenter: Drew Bent (Anthropic)

## Learning objective
- Identify the major capabilities and limitations of current generative AI (focus: LLMs such as Claude)

Framing: treat it like **getting to know a new colleague**. Knowing their strengths and limits helps you collaborate.

## Capabilities
1. **Versatility with language**
   - crafting emails that **capture your voice**
   - condensing long reports into clear summaries
   - translating between languages
   - explaining complex topics across fields (from microbiology to marketing strategy)
2. **Switching tasks without extra training**: the same system can write poetry, brainstorm a birthday party, explain quantum computing and analyse quarterly business trends, all through plain conversation.
3. **Maintaining the thread of a conversation**: it remembers what was said earlier and builds on it (e.g. you mention a project deadline in passing and refer back later; it understands, much like a human partner).
4. **Connecting to external tools and information**: many LLMs can search the web, process files, or use other applications, which greatly expands what they can help with.

## Limitations
1. **Knowledge cutoff date (bounded by training data)**
   - No innate knowledge of the world after the training cutoff. Example: a November 2024 cutoff means no training data after November 2024.
   - Analogy: someone who went on a retreat without internet on a given date does not know what happened after.
   - Needs tools such as **web search** for recent developments.
2. **Hallucinations**
   - Training does **not verify every fact** in the data, so models can learn and reproduce inaccuracies, and can make mistakes when piecing information together.
   - Hallucination = AI **confidently stating something plausible but incorrect**.
   - Unlike search engines, which **retrieve existing documents**, LLMs **generate** responses from statistical patterns.
   - Analogy: a friend telling a story with total confidence but the details completely wrong.
3. **Context window limits**
   - Every LLM has a maximum amount it can consider in one interaction.
   - When exceeded, information outside the window is lost, **usually first in, first out** (the oldest drops off).
   - Can limit processing of large documents or remembering a whole conversation.
4. **Non-determinism (unpredictability)**
   - Unlike traditional software (same input → same output), LLMs are **non-deterministic by default**: ask twice, get slightly different answers.
   - Cause: **probabilistic decisions** about what text comes next, plus settings developers can adjust.
   - Good for **brainstorming and diverse ideas**; needs awareness when **consistency or accuracy** is critical.
   - Some interfaces let you control randomness via a setting usually called **temperature**.
5. **Complex reasoning**
   - Historically weak on complex multi-step **mathematical or logical** problems.
   - Newer **reasoning / "extended thinking" models** that think step by step show strong progress.
6. **Lack of access to specific data or tools**
   - Even with tool use, a model may lack the data source or specialised tool a task needs.
   - Analogy: a brilliant colleague who **cannot access your company's internal database**; no matter how smart, their help is limited.
   - If the model does not have the data or tool, **do not be surprised it cannot answer**.

### Diagram: context window overflow and knowledge cutoff
```
 CONTEXT WINDOW FULL: oldest content falls out first (first in, first out)

   lost ◄── [ msg 1 ][ msg 2 ][ msg 3 ][ msg 4 ][ msg 5 ] ◄── new message
             └────────────── window size ─────────────┘

 KNOWLEDGE CUTOFF

   ──────── training data ────────┤ cutoff ├─────── the world keeps going ───►
          model knows this              model does not know this
                                        (needs web search or other tools)
```

## Where the field is heading
- Researchers are addressing limitations through:
  - **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**: connecting models to external knowledge and data sources
  - expanding **tool use**
  - improving **reasoning**
- Some limitations will likely remain for the foreseeable future, though we do not know exactly which.

## Complementary strengths (the key message)
| Humans bring | AI brings |
|---|---|
| critical thinking | speed |
| judgment | scale |
| creativity | pattern recognition |
| ethical oversight | processing vast amounts of information |

- The most effective applications **combine these complementary strengths**.
- The balance **will shift as the technology evolves**, so continued learning and experimentation matter.
- Knowing what AI can and cannot do is essential to fluency and directly informs **Delegation**.

## Key takeaways (from the lesson page, covering lessons 4 and 5)
- Generative AI creates new content (text, images, code) rather than just analysing data
- Made possible by: architectural breakthroughs (transformer), vast training data, much more compute
- Two training stages: **pre-training** (patterns across billions of examples) and **fine-tuning** (following instructions, helpful responses)
- Capabilities: versatility, conversational awareness, connecting to external tools
- Limitations: knowledge cutoff, hallucinations, context window constraints, complex reasoning challenges
- Best results combine human and AI strengths, with humans providing critical thinking, judgment, creativity and ethical oversight

# 06. A closer look at Delegation

Video: ~6 min

## Learning objectives
- Understand Delegation and its three components: **Problem Awareness, Platform Awareness, Task Delegation**
- Recognise when and how to delegate to AI effectively
- Develop awareness of task, platform and mode considerations

## Where Delegation fits
- Of the four aims of AI Fluency (effective, efficient, ethical, safe), Delegation is **primarily about the first two: effective and efficient**.
- Core question: **what work is to be done, what should you do yourself, and what is better suited to AI?**
- Sounds simple but is **surprisingly nuanced**. It requires knowing both what you are trying to accomplish and what you and the AI can each realistically do.
- It is about **breaking complex work into manageable pieces** and deciding who handles which part, whether through **automation, augmentation or agency**.

## The surprise: good delegation is not mainly about AI
> The cornerstone of good delegation is **your own expertise** and your understanding of what you are trying to accomplish.

> "The most effective AI collaborators are **experts in their fields first and AI delegators second**."

## Component 1: Problem Awareness
*The ability to clearly define your goals and understand what work is needed **before** bringing AI in.*

Questions to answer first:
- What exactly are you trying to accomplish, create or solve?
- What is your vision, what are the key project goals?
- **What does success look like** to you?
- What kind of thinking and work is needed to get there? Look for:
  - areas that are **simple but time-consuming**
  - areas of **uncertainty** where a trusted thinking partner would help
  - areas of **ignorance** where you need more data
  - areas needing **critical judgment**

Every project and domain differs; the course cannot make you an expert in your problem space. But **without a clear understanding of goals and the work involved, even the most advanced AI will not get you there**. You will struggle to work with AI, **evaluate its outputs**, or know **when human intervention is needed**.

## Component 2: Platform Awareness
*A working knowledge of available AI systems and their specific capabilities and limitations.*

- Different systems offer **vastly different capabilities**, and the field changes almost daily.
- Delegation is **not about finding one perfect system**; it is about knowing the strengths and limits of the options available.
- Questions:
  - Have you checked **which models perform best** for the kind of work you have in mind?
  - Do you know which systems prioritise **speed over depth**, or **accuracy over creativity**?
- Best approach: **hands-on**. Experiment with different systems as often as you can and build insight from personal experience.

## Component 3: Task Delegation
*The strategic process of dividing work between humans and AI to use the strengths of each.*

Questions:
- Which parts of the workflow would benefit from **automation**?
- Where would **augmentation** create more value than either working alone or fully automating?
- Are there **critical judgment areas that should stay exclusively human** and not be delegated at all?
- What **routine interactions can AI agents handle** on your behalf (agency)?

Outcome: an informed decision for each part of the work, one of four buckets:
1. **Automated** by AI
2. **Human-AI collaboration** (augmentation)
3. **Reserved exclusively for humans**
4. **Handled by AI agents** acting on your behalf

### Diagram: the delegation flow
```
 1. PROBLEM AWARENESS      2. PLATFORM AWARENESS      3. TASK DELEGATION
 ─────────────────────     ──────────────────────     ──────────────────────────
 what am I solving?        which AI systems exist?    split the work into:
 what is success?     +    what does each do well  ─►   ▸ AI automates
 what work is needed?      and badly?                   ▸ human + AI together
                           (learn by trying)            ▸ human only (judgment)
                                                        ▸ AI agent on my behalf

      "experts in their field first, AI delegators second"
```

## Recap
| Component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Problem Awareness | Understand your goals and the problem |
| Platform Awareness | Understand AI capabilities and limitations |
| Task Delegation | Strategically divide the work |

- Effective collaboration is **not "handing over the wheel and calling it a day"**. It is about thoughtful choices that use the unique strengths of human and artificial intelligence.
- The goal is **not to automate everything** but to create the most effective human-AI partnership for the task.
- Requires **both domain expertise and an understanding of AI capabilities**.

## Exercise: Analyse a task with an AI assistant
1. Pick a small task (draft an email, outline a presentation, plan a meeting or event).
2. Start a chat with Claude, e.g. *"Hi Claude, I'm preparing how to [task] and want to discuss with you what a delegation plan may look like for figuring out which parts I should delegate to an AI like you vs. not. Can you help me with this?"*
3. Explore together:
   - What is the overall **vision**? What does a **good result** look like?
   - What are the different **bits of work** needed?
   - Which bits need **human expertise, creativity or judgment**?
4. **Have a real back-and-forth conversation**, not just lists of statements. Each of you may see something the other does not.
5. Build a simple **delegation plan** using both your strengths and the AI's.

# 08. A closer look at Description

Video: ~4 min

## Learning objectives
- Communicate your intentions to AI systems effectively
- Recognise the importance of clear, purposeful communication
- Build skill in the three types of Description: **Product, Process, Performance**

## What Description is
- Communicating with AI is **the core of most human-AI interaction**.
- Description goes **far beyond writing clever prompts**. It covers:
  - explaining tasks, asking questions, providing context, guiding the interaction
  - **steering a conversation that is going wrong**
  - **guiding the AI's thought process or reasoning**
  - building a **thinking environment** where both you and the AI can do your best work
- Metaphor: a **bridge between your intentions and the AI's capabilities**.
- Output quality often depends on how clearly you describe what you want. Analogy: asking someone to "make dinner" vs. giving a **detailed recipe with ingredients and instructions**.
- **AI cannot read your mind.** Do not assume it knows what you want; explain every relevant detail. **Do not make AI guess.**

## 1. Product Description: *what* you want
*The ability to clearly define what you want the AI to create or provide.*

Questions to cover:
- What is the **context** for this work?
- What exactly do you want the AI **to do**?
- What **format** should the output take?
- Who is the **audience**, and what **style** is appropriate?

Set **explicit requirements** and give the AI the information it needs.

## 2. Process Description: *how* the AI should approach it
*The ability to guide how the AI approaches your request.*

- Sometimes **how** the AI tackles a job matters **as much as or more than** the end goal (just as you might want a colleague to use your specific method).
- Three ways to give process guidance:
  - **general guidance**, like a manual
  - **step-by-step instructions**, like a cookbook
  - **demonstration through examples** ("here's how I'd do it")
- The AI already has extensive training; you add **context specific to your situation**. Ask:
  - Is there **specific data** it should draw on?
  - Are there **specific issues** to address, or a **particular order**?
  - A particular **style of analysis**, **workflow** or **technique** to use?

## 3. Performance Description: *how the AI should behave* with you
*The ability to define the behavioural aspects of an AI interaction.*

> "If you take one thing from this course, it should be this: AI tools are **not databases or vending machines**. They are **interactive systems that can behave differently in different contexts**, much like people."

Ask yourself first: **what kind of thinking partner do I need right now?**
- **Narrowing** toward a specific answer, or **exploring** multiple possibilities?
- Should the AI **challenge my assumptions** or **follow my lead**?
- **Expansive detail** or **concise**?
- **Explain why** it answers a certain way, or **just give the answer**?

### Diagram: the three kinds of description
```
 "make dinner"  ──────────────►  a full recipe
  (vague)                         (what + how + how to work with me)

                ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 PRODUCT     ─► │ WHAT should come out?       output, format, audience, style      │
 PROCESS     ─► │ HOW should it work?         data, steps, order, method, examples │
 PERFORMANCE ─► │ HOW should it act with me?  concise/detailed, challenge/follow   │
                └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## Summary
| Type | Question it answers | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Product | What do I want? | output, format, audience, style |
| Process | How should the AI work on it? | data to use, steps, order, method, examples |
| Performance | How should the AI behave with me? | concise vs. detailed, challenging vs. supportive, explain vs. answer |

- Developing Description turns AI from a **generic assistant** into a **finely tuned thinking partner**.
- **Clear communication up front saves time** and gives better results.

## Exercise: Bad Prompt Makeover (10 min)
1. Ask Claude to **challenge you with poorly written prompts** to improve.
2. Improve each one using:
   - clear **product** description (what exactly you want)
   - **process** guidance (how Claude should approach it)
   - **performance** specs (how Claude should behave)
3. Discuss before/after with Claude and ask for feedback on how your version helps it respond better.
4. After ~5 min, **switch roles**: give Claude bad prompts to fix. Notice **what information Claude adds** and **how it organises it**.

# 09. Effective prompting techniques (Deep Dive 2)

Video: ~11 min · Examples use Claude, but apply to most AI systems

## Learning objectives
- Understand what prompt engineering is and why it matters
- Apply **six foundational prompting techniques**
- Identify patterns that lead to successful interactions
- Troubleshoot and refine prompts when responses miss

## What prompting is
- Not as technical as some guides make it seem. **Prompting is how we apply the Description competency in practice**: what we want, how we want it done, how we want to interact throughout.
- Analogy: explaining a task to a **helpful new colleague** who is eager but needs clear directions and expectations.
- **Prompt engineering** = the practice of designing effective instructions for AI systems: crafting questions and context so the AI understands exactly what you want.

### Same as human communication
- be clear · give relevant context · give concrete examples

### Different from human communication
- be **more explicit** about things a human would naturally infer
- accommodate the AI's **limited context window**
- sometimes (depending on the AI) use **specific formatting** that machines process easily

Best practices **change as models evolve**; what works today may differ tomorrow. **Experimentation is key.**

## The six foundational techniques

### 1. Give context
Be specific about **what** you want, **why** you want it, and (surprisingly important) **who you are**.

- Vague: *"Tell me about climate change."* Leaves Claude guessing about your interests, knowledge level, depth, geography, time span.
- Better: *"Explain three major impacts of climate change on agriculture in tropical regions, with examples from the past decade."*
- Best (adds why and how you will use it): *...plus: "I'm preparing for a job interview at an agricultural research lab in Indonesia. I have a degree in ecology but no specific knowledge of climate change. Write a summary of key concepts that would help me speak intelligently in the interview."*
- This is background we naturally give people but forget to give AI.

### Diagram: anatomy of the improved climate prompt
```
 ┌ WHAT ─── Explain three major impacts of climate change on agriculture
 │          in tropical regions, with examples from the past decade.
 ├ WHY ──── I'm preparing for a job interview at an agricultural research
 │          lab in Indonesia.
 ├ WHO ──── I have a degree in ecology but no specific knowledge of
 │          climate change.
 └ OUTPUT ─ Write a summary of key concepts that would help me speak
            intelligently in the interview.
```

### 2. Show examples (few-shot / n-shot prompting)
- "n" = number of examples. Really just **showing the AI examples to emulate**.
- Prompt: *"Please convert this technical statement to plain language: The platform implements end-to-end encryption protocols to safeguard data integrity."*
- **Try without examples first**; Claude may already do it well.
- If you need a specific style that is easier to show than explain:
  - *Original:* "The quantum algorithm exhibits quadratic speedup." → *Plain:* "The new method solves problems roughly twice as fast as previous methods."
  - *Original:* "The interface leverages intuitive design paradigms." → *Plain:* "The design is easy to understand and use."
  - "Now please convert this complex technical manual to plain language."
- **Cover the full diversity** of cases and styles in your examples so the AI understands the breadth of the pattern.

### 3. Specify output constraints
- Format, length, programming language, even the colour of buttons on a page.
- Example: *"Create a clean, modern, single-page art portfolio website. Include these main sections: hero, about me, skills, portfolio, projects, experience, and contact. Make the navigation menu sticky and responsive with a hamburger menu on mobile. Use a sunset colour palette and add a dark/light mode toggle in the navigation."*
- Helps the AI **structure its response** to match expectations.

### 4. Break complex tasks into steps (chain-of-thought prompting)
- Like asking a friend to do something without saying how: they may not do it your way.
- Instead of *"Analyse this quarterly sales data"*: *"I'd like to analyse this quarterly sales data. Please approach this by (1) looking through our sales records to identify the top-performing products, (2) comparing current quarter results to the previous quarter, (3) highlighting any unusual trends or patterns, and then (4) suggesting possible reasons for these trends."*
- Often **unnecessary for simple tasks**, and reasoning / extended-thinking models increasingly do step-by-step reasoning themselves, but you can still guide it.
- Rule of thumb: **the more variance in ways to do the task well, or the more it relies on your domain experience, the more you should translate that knowledge into the prompt.**

### 5. Ask the AI to think first
- Give the AI **space to work through its process before executing**, for more thorough, considered responses.
- Example addition: *"Before answering, please think through this problem carefully. Consider the different factors involved, potential constraints, and various approaches before recommending the best solution."*
- Reasoning / extended-thinking models do this by default; for others you can prompt it.
- **Thinking must come *before* the answer, not after.** Explaining after the fact does not improve quality, just like acting first and justifying later is different from thinking before acting.
- Side benefit: you can **see where the AI goes astray**, which shows where to add more guidance.

### Diagram: why thinking must come first
```
 ✔  think ──► answer       the thinking shapes and improves the answer
 ✘  answer ──► explain     explaining afterwards does not improve it
```

### 6. Define the AI's role, style or tone
- Specifying **expertise level, perspective, communication style** changes both the interaction and the final result. In short: **who do you want the AI to act as?**
- *"Please explain how rainbows form from the perspective of an experienced science teacher speaking to a bright 10-year-old who's interested in science."*
- Good for brainstorming and feedback. Can be a general role or a **specific figure's persona** (e.g. Richard Feynman for physics explanations).
- *"As a UX design expert, review this website wireframe and suggest three improvements focusing on user navigation and accessibility."*

## The "secret weapon": ask the AI to improve your prompt
- Perhaps the most powerful technique. Describe your situation and ask Claude to improve or write the prompt:
  - *"I'm trying to get you, Claude, to help me with [goal]. I'm not sure how to phrase my request to get the best results. Can you help me craft an effective prompt for this?"*
- This is where AI assistants **vary most** in performance, so experiment with models (part of **Platform Awareness** in Delegation).

## Iterating and troubleshooting
Prompting is **iterative and experimental**; the first attempt will not always be perfect, and that is expected. When a response misses:
- add **more specificity or context**
- provide **examples** of desired output
- **break the task** into smaller steps
- try a **different technique or combination**
- ask for **variations**: *"Can you give me three different versions of this?"*
- request **different formats**: *"Instead of a paragraph, could you present this in an interactive artifact?"* (Artifacts are Claude outputs that can be easier to understand or more engaging.)
- **check confidence** on factual questions: *"How confident are you about this answer?"*
- **reset the conversation**: a fresh start sometimes beats correcting a conversation that has gone off track

Use each interaction as feedback for the next prompt; over time you build intuition.

### Diagram: the iteration loop
```
 prompt ──► response ──► good enough? ── yes ──► done
   ▲                          │ no
   │                          ▼
   └──── adjust: more context · examples · smaller steps · other technique
                · "give me 3 versions" · different format · "how confident are you?"
                · or start a fresh conversation
```

## Patterns that work
- a clear **task overview** statement up front
- **format specifications** and **examples**
- **explicit constraints** or requirements
- **rich, relevant background** information

## Common mistakes
- assuming Claude **can read your mind**
- **overloading** one prompt or conversation with multiple **unrelated tasks**
- being **too vague about what success looks like**
- **not giving feedback** on previous responses

## Recap
- Effective prompting = **timeless human communication principles + AI-specific techniques**.
- Six techniques + the secret weapon = a toolkit for applying **Description**.
- **Iteration and practice** are the key to fast improvement.
- Prompt engineering evolves: as models improve **some specific techniques become less necessary**, but the **principles of good communication stay relevant**.

# 10. A closer look at Discernment

Video: ~5 min

## Learning objectives
- Evaluate AI outputs and processes thoughtfully
- Build critical-thinking skills for AI interactions
- Identify and address quality concerns

## What Discernment is
- Evaluating AI **outputs, processes and behaviours**: your **quality control system** for AI collaboration.
- The **flip side of Description**: Description communicates what you want; Discernment decides **whether what you got actually meets your needs**.
- It helps you:
  - tell when outputs are **valuable vs. problematic**
  - recognise **strengths and limitations**
  - decide when outputs are **ready to use or need more work**
- Requires **two things**:
  1. **Domain expertise**: knowing enough to judge quality
  2. **Understanding how AI systems work**, including their typical shortcomings
- Even the most advanced systems make **reasoning errors, factual mistakes, or act unexpectedly**. Your discernment is an **essential safeguard**.

## 1. Product Discernment: the output
*The ability to judge the accuracy and value of AI-created output.*

Ask:
- Is it **factually accurate**?
- Is it **appropriate for my audience and purpose**?
- Is it **coherent and well structured**?
- Does it **meet my requirements**?
- Does it **add value / solve the problem** I intended?

(Page summary: accuracy, appropriateness, coherence, relevance.)

## 2. Process Discernment: how the AI got there
*The ability to judge the quality and effectiveness of the AI's process.*

Watch for:
- **logical errors**
- **lapses in attention**
- **inappropriate steps**
- **getting stuck** on one small detail or interpretation and failing to consider alternatives
- **circular reasoning**

Example: you are expanding one of **five outline options** the AI offered. After several rounds of ideation you notice **elements of ideas you rejected are being reinserted**. Spotting that is process discernment.

- Ensures you and the AI **stay in sync** and the AI is guided toward your vision of success.
- **Especially important for complex tasks** where the correct answer is not obvious; there, **trust in the process is everything**.

### Diagram: process vs. performance
```
 PROCESS      = the work the AI is doing      (reasoning, steps, focus, loops)
 PERFORMANCE  = how it works WITH YOU          (communication, length, responsiveness)
```

## 3. Performance Discernment: how the AI behaves with you
*The ability to judge the quality of the human-AI interaction.*

- Subtle distinction: **process = the work the AI is doing; performance = how well it interacts with you while doing it.**
- Ask:
  - Is there a **better way for it to communicate** with me for ease and productivity?
  - Is it giving the information I need **in a helpful way**?
  - Does it **respond well to feedback and direction**?
  - Is the interaction **efficient or unnecessarily complex**?
- Example: is it **asking too many questions** when you need concise answers, or **too brief** when you need comprehensive information?

## Discernment does not end with evaluation: give feedback
Effective feedback when you find a problem:
1. **Specify what** the problem is
2. **Explain why** it is a problem
3. Give **concrete suggestions** for improvement
4. **Revise your instructions or examples**

- When discernment flags a problem, **better description is often the solution**.
- But not always: sometimes you must **rethink Delegation**, because you are using the **wrong tool** or approaching the problem the **wrong way entirely**.

### Diagram: what to do when discernment finds a problem
```
 DISCERNMENT flags a problem
        │
        ├──► usually: improve DESCRIPTION
        │        say what is wrong ▸ why ▸ suggest a fix ▸ revise prompt/examples
        │
        └──► sometimes: rethink DELEGATION
                 wrong tool, or wrong approach to the problem
```

## Recap
| Description (mirror) | Discernment |
|---|---|
| Product: what I want | Product: is the output good? |
| Process: how to approach it | Process: was the approach sound? |
| Performance: how to behave | Performance: was the interaction effective? |

- Description + Discernment = a **continuous loop of instruction and evaluation that drives quality**.
- Discernment keeps AI collaboration **guided by human judgment**; even the most advanced systems benefit from human oversight.

## Exercise: Expert Discernment in your domain
1. Return to the topic you know well from lesson 3's "Explore something you love" exercise.
2. In a new chat, ask Claude for **three different explanations or analyses** of one aspect (e.g. three explanations of depth of field in photography; three analyses of fermentation in cooking; three perspectives on a historical event).
3. Evaluate each:
   - **Product**: most accurate? any errors or misconceptions? right level of detail for a learner?
   - **Process**: logical reasoning? gaps? sensible connections between concepts?
   - **Performance**: attentive to your question and responsive to feedback? terminology appropriate? how do tone and style affect clarity?
4. Feedback: tell Claude **specifically why** the strongest is effective and what makes the weakest problematic; co-create an improved version.

(For a more playful workout, see the "Game Night" activities in the course's Additional Activities lesson.)

## The exercise: execute the project you planned
(The project plan from lesson 7, "Project planning and Delegation". The course page calls it "Lesson 5" using its own numbering.)

### Step 1: Review your project plan
- Pull up the plan and re-check your delegation decisions (human expertise vs. AI capability vs. collaboration).

   - **take responsibility** for the final output (bridge to Diligence)

Repeat for each task until the project is complete.

```
   │                                                                     ▼
 Refine  ◄──── Discern (product / process / performance) ◄──── AI response
   │
   └─► Integrate your expertise → final decision → own the result
```

# 12. A closer look at Diligence

Video: ~7 min · Lesson time: ~20 min

## Learning objectives
- Understand the ethical implications of AI collaboration
- Understand the importance of transparency
- Recognise your responsibility for AI interactions and outputs

## Where Diligence fits
- The other three Ds are mainly about **effective and efficient**. Diligence focuses mostly on **ethical and safe**, which are just as crucial.
- At heart: **taking responsibility for your AI interactions**, making AI use not only productive but **rigorous, transparent and accountable**.
- Broader questions it raises (especially in professional settings):
  - What are the **implications** of working with this AI?
  - **Who might be affected** by what is created, by the collaboration itself, or by any **missed inaccuracies**?
  - **Who has access to the data** used to produce this output?
  - How do I make sure the interaction and outcome **align with ethical standards and values**?
- Analogy: **driving a car**. We do not only care about getting from A to B efficiently; we also consider safety, follow traffic rules, and stay aware of how our driving affects others. AI interactions **do not exist in a vacuum**.

### Diagram: the three kinds of diligence
```
 ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐
 │ CREATION              │   │ TRANSPARENCY          │   │ DEPLOYMENT            │
 │ choosing and using AI │   │ being open about it   │   │ owning the output     │
 │                       │   │                       │   │                       │
 │ right system?         │   │ who needs to know?    │   │ facts verified?       │
 │ what data am I        │   │ how and when?         │   │ bias checked?         │
 │ sharing? who sees it? │   │ how much detail?      │   │ usage rights ok?      │
 │ allowed by policy?    │   │                       │   │ can I stand behind it?│
 └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘
        like driving: not just getting there, but safely and with care for others
```

## 1. Creation Diligence: choosing systems and how you use them
*The ability to be critical and intentional about which AI systems you choose and how you work with them.*

Questions:
- How is this system **trained and built**? What **data** was used?
- **Who owns the data** I am inputting? **Who may access it** once shared?
- How am I **protecting the privacy and security** of myself and others?
- What **other impacts** does this system have?
- Does this interaction align with my **personal and professional values** and my **organisation's policies**?

Example: **before sharing sensitive company information** with an AI assistant, check whether the service has **appropriate data protection policies** and whether **your organisation permits** such sharing.

## 2. Transparency Diligence: being open about AI's role
*The ability to be open and accurate about AI interaction with everyone who needs to know.*

- Settings (personal, academic, creative, professional) have **different disclosure expectations**, but **the responsibility is on each of us to understand and meet them**.
- Ask:
  - **Who needs to know** about AI's role in this work?
  - **How and when** should I communicate it?
  - **What level of detail** makes sense?
- Transparency is not just following rules and regulations; it is about **maintaining trust and respect in relationships**. People have **a right to know** when AI played a significant role in content or in decisions that affect them.
- Example: you used AI to help draft a **team proposal**. Telling colleagues **which parts were AI-assisted** allows more honest collaboration and keeps everyone aligned.

## 3. Deployment Diligence: owning what you share
*The ability to take informed responsibility for the outputs you use or share after creating them with AI.*

- AI makes mistakes. When you share AI-generated content, **you, not the AI, are ultimately responsible** for its accuracy and appropriateness.
- That means: **verifying facts, checking for biases, ensuring accuracy and usage rights**, and any other checks needed so you can **stand behind** what you share.
- Example: a **journalist** using AI to draft an article must verify every fact and source and meet **every journalistic standard**, the **same standards as if they had written it entirely themselves**.

## Navigating it in practice
- Not always straightforward; contexts and stakeholders have different expectations and standards.
- Develop **personal guidelines** aligned with your own ethics and values.
- In professional contexts, learn your **organisational policies and industry standards**.
- **Legal and regulatory frameworks for AI are still emerging** and will keep evolving; **staying informed** is part of diligence.

## Recap
| Component | Core responsibility |
|---|---|
| Creation | be thoughtful about **which systems** and **how** you use them |
| Transparency | be **honest about AI's role** with everyone who needs to know |
| Deployment | be **accountable** for what you use or share (verify and vouch) |

> "We all want AI that is fair and safe and of benefit to our society. Our own behaviours play a key role in making this happen."

## Exercise: Create a Diligence Statement (~14 min)
A **diligence statement** = a transparent acknowledgment of AI's role in your work plus your commitment to responsibility for the final output.

**Example template from the course:**
> "In creating this [document/project/content], I collaborated with [AI assistant name] to assist with [specific tasks: drafting, research, editing, etc.]. I affirm that all AI-generated and co-created content underwent thorough review and evaluation. The final output accurately reflects my understanding, expertise, and intended meaning. While AI assistance was instrumental in the process, I maintain full responsibility for the content, its accuracy, and its presentation. This disclosure is made in the spirit of transparency and to acknowledge the role of AI in the creation process."

Steps:
1. **Understand** diligence statements (3 min).
2. **Reflect** on your project (5 min):
   - *Creation*: which AI systems did you choose and why? what data did you share? any privacy, security or ethical considerations?
   - *Transparency*: who is the audience? what disclosure expectations might they have? how specifically did AI contribute to each part?
   - *Deployment*: how did you verify accuracy and appropriateness? how did you make sure the output meets your standards? what responsibility are you taking?
3. **Draft** with Claude (6 min), covering: systems used, how AI contributed, your review process, your assertion of responsibility, context-specific considerations (academic, professional, etc.).
4. **Add it to the project** (footer, appendix or metadata).

These apply across all **three modes** of interaction:
- **Automation**: AI performs specific tasks following your instructions
- **Augmentation**: you and AI collaborate as thinking partners
- **Agency**: AI is configured to act independently on your behalf

## Essential insights to carry forward
- **Delegation**: your **expertise and judgment remain the foundation** of effective AI use. The most powerful outcomes come from spotting where you and AI can **build on each other's strengths through iterative collaboration**, creating outcomes **neither could achieve alone**.
- **Description**: clear communication **bridges your intentions and AI capabilities**. Often the most effective instructions are **not elaborate prompts but thoughtful conversations** that provide context, examples and feedback.
- **Discernment**: critical evaluation of AI output is a **non-negotiable responsibility**. It protects against AI limitations and enables results neither side could easily create alone.
- **Diligence**: **you remain accountable** for what you do with AI and its impact on others. **Transparency builds trust and integrity.**

## On developing fluency
- These competencies are **not mastered overnight**; they grow through **practice**: delegate, describe, discern, apply diligence.
- **The goal is not perfection**; it is continually developing **awareness, commitment and an intentional approach**.
- The framework is designed to apply across the broad range of generative AI assistants and to **stay relevant as AI evolves**, but it is up to us to keep applying it.

## Closing message
- AI systems are powerful but **not silver bullets or magical solutions**. They are **only as useful and safe as we enable them to be**.
- So:
  - **invest in your own expertise** and **stay aware of technological change** so you can delegate appropriately
  - **practise description and discernment**, the back-and-forth that turns AI from automation machines into **genuine thinking partners**
  - **take responsibility** for how you work with AI and what you create together
- **Be effective and efficient. Be ethical and safe.**
